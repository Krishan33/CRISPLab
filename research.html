Research

Five connected lines of work.

Unified by one question: how do we build materials that read their environment and respond with function? Expand any line below for the underlying science.

Protein–polymer hybrid materials

Bioconjugation strategies that graft synthetic polymers onto proteins and enzymes, tuning stability, circulation time, and self-assembly while preserving biological function — bridging polymer chemistry with structural biology.

Read more +Read less –

The goal is to make functional biomolecules more robust and more programmable without losing what makes them work. Grafting synthetic polymers onto proteins and enzymes through site-selective bioconjugation, rather than random labeling, can extend circulation time, protect against denaturation during storage and transport, and drive controlled self-assembly into higher-order structures — while respecting the native fold and active site of the biomolecule being modified.

Stimuli-responsive nanogels

Crosslinked polymer networks engineered to swell, collapse, or disassemble on cue — pH, temperature, enzymes, or oxidative stress — holding cargo until the target environment unlocks it.

Read more +Read less –

These networks are built by controlled radical polymerization (RAFT, ATRP) so mesh size, crosslink density, and the responsive trigger can be tuned independently. A network that collapses cleanly on a small drop in pH in solution behaves very differently once it has to survive a biological environment — part of the work is translating that clean response into something that still functions in vivo, including formulations that use deep eutectic solvents to stabilize sensitive optical and therapeutic payloads.

CRISPR / Cas9 RNP delivery

Polymeric nanocapsules that protect Cas9–sgRNA ribonucleoproteins and carry them across mucosal barriers, with a focus on transmucosal gene editing for cystic fibrosis.

Read more +Read less –

Ribonucleoprotein complexes are fragile — activity drops off outside a narrow window of pH, ionic strength, and shear. Encapsulating a Cas9–sgRNA RNP in a polymeric nanocapsule means protecting that complex through formulation, storage, and transport across a mucosal barrier, then releasing it intact once inside the target cell. Cystic fibrosis, where the airway mucosa is both the barrier and the target tissue, is the disease context driving most of this work.

RNP encapsulation mucosal delivery gene editing

Chemoenzymatic nanoreactors

Enzyme-loaded nanogels decorated with metal nanoclusters that run cascade reactions in confinement, coupling biocatalysis with controlled polymerization and tunable release.

Read more +Read less –

Confining an enzyme and a metal nanocluster inside the same nanogel lets a cascade reaction run in one pot instead of several purification steps, and protects the enzyme from the conditions the reaction itself creates. The harder engineering problem isn't any single reaction — it's compartmentalization: getting substrate in, handing an intermediate to the next active site, and getting product out, while keeping the network stable enough to be reused.

Theranostics & digital twins

Sensor-coupled, imaging-active platforms paired with physics-informed models that predict nanocarrier transport, closing the loop between what a material does and what we can measure.

Read more +Read less –

A nanocarrier's behavior in the body depends on dozens of interacting parameters — size, charge, crosslink density, the biological environment it meets first. Rather than screening all of that by trial and error, this line builds physics-informed models that predict how a given formulation will behave before it's synthesized, developed in parallel with Akshar Innovations, an AI-driven nanocarrier prediction venture — closing the loop between formulation design and prediction.
