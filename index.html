What I work on.

Four lines, one question: how do you get something fragile, intact, to the place it has to act?

01

CRISPR/Cas9 RNP delivery

Polymeric nanocapsules that protect Cas9–sgRNA complexes through formulation, storage and transport across mucosal barriers, then release them intact inside the target cell. Cystic fibrosis is the driving disease context.

RNP encapsulationmucosal deliverygene editing
02

Stimuli-responsive nanogels

Crosslinked networks engineered to swell, collapse or disassemble on cue — pH, temperature, redox, enzymatic activity — so that mesh size, degradation and the trigger itself can be tuned independently.

RAFT / ATRPthermoresponsivedegradable
03

Protein–polymer hybrids and nanoreactors

Site-selective bioconjugation that grafts synthetic polymers onto proteins and enzymes without disturbing the active site, and enzyme-loaded nanogels that run chemoenzymatic cascades in confinement.

bioconjugationenzyme stabilisationcascade catalysis
04

Predictive formulation and digital twins

Physics-informed models that predict how size, charge and surface chemistry translate into delivery efficiency, before a batch is ever synthesised. Developed in parallel with Akshar Innovations.

(02)

A chemist who formulates.

I did my PhD at the University of Delhi, then moved to Inha University in South Korea on an NRF fellowship, where I ran a project in my own name for the first time. Spain followed: Juan de la Cierva at POLYMAT, then MSCA COFUND, then an individual Marie Curie fellowship, and now a Ramón y Cajal contract taking me to Valladolid to start a group of my own.

Most of that time has been spent on one stubborn problem. A gene editor, an enzyme or an exosome will do exactly what it is supposed to in a tube and almost nothing in a patient, because the journey destroys it. The chemistry that fixes that is not glamorous — crosslink density, mesh size, which cysteine you modify, whether the thing survives freeze-drying — but it is where the therapy is won or lost.

Alongside the academic work I co-founded Akshar Innovations, which predicts nanocarrier behaviour computationally, and I lead drug delivery for a precision-therapeutics venture. Both exist for the same reason: formulation decisions made by trial and error are slow, and most of what is learned never leaves the lab notebook.

How a project runs.

The order matters more than the techniques. Most failed formulations were decided before anything was synthesised.

01 / 04

Define the failure

Establish what actually destroys the cargo — and where — before choosing a material.

02 / 04

Design and predict

Pick the chemistry, and model the outcome first so the screen is small and deliberate.

03 / 04

Make and interrogate

Synthesise, then test the thing that would disprove it rather than the thing that confirms it.

04 / 04

Hand it forward

Protocols and parameters written so the result survives the person who produced it.
